Module 3 Surveillance Literacy

3. Count vs. Rate vs. Leading-Cause Rank vs. Trend

Why absolute death counts, crude rates, leading-cause ordinal ranks, and annual percentage trends convey fundamentally different information about public health burden.

30-Second Takeaway

Leading-cause rank depends entirely on what other causes kill people at that age. In youth, chronic diseases are rare, so suicide ranks high (#2) despite a lower absolute rate (~10.5/100k) than in older adults (~18–22/100k) where heart disease and cancer dominate.

Terms Not to Conflate

Term A Term B Scientific Distinction
Absolute Count Population Rate per 100k Count is the total number of events; rate divides count by population size to measure incidence.
Leading Cause Rank Absolute Mortality Rate Rank is relative to all other competing causes of death in that cohort; rate is the frequency per 100,000 people.
Permitted Claims & Primary Citation Locators

Suicide ranks as the second leading cause of death among U.S. adolescents and young adults aged 10–24 because competing causes of mortality are minimal in this cohort.

[DRAFT - OPEN] CLM-RATE-VS-RANK-LOGIC: A high leading-cause rank (e.g., suicide as the 2nd leading cause of death among ages 10–14 or 15–24) does not imply a higher absolute mortality rate than in older adult groups; it occurs because overall all-cause mortality (such as chronic disease and cancer) is extremely low in youth cohorts.
Primary Locator: WISQARS Leading Causes of Death Visualization System & Explanatory Guides
Underlying Sources: [SRC-CDC-WISQARS-2024] [SRC-CDC-NVSS-2022-FINAL]

Module 4 Epidemiological Metrics

4. Crude vs. Age-Adjusted Rates

The mathematical necessity of direct age standardization, how differing demographic age pyramids distort crude comparisons, and how the 2000 U.S. Standard Population is applied.

30-Second Takeaway

Crude rates divide raw deaths by total population. Age-adjusted rates apply age-specific rates to a standard population (U.S. 2000 benchmark) so that older populations (like Florida) and younger populations (like Utah or Alaska) can be fairly compared.

Terms Not to Conflate

Term A Term B Scientific Distinction
Crude Mortality Rate Age-Adjusted Mortality Rate Crude rate reflects actual unadjusted events in the specific local population; age-adjusted rate standardizes for age structure.
Direct Age Standardization Indirect Standardization Direct standardization applies observed age-specific rates to a single benchmark population.
Permitted Claims & Primary Citation Locators

Age-adjusted rates eliminate the confounding effect of age distribution differences when comparing suicide risk between different states or demographic groups.

[DRAFT - OPEN] CLM-CRUDE-VS-AGEADJ: Crude death rates measure actual observed events divided by the unweighted population, whereas age-adjusted rates standardize the population distribution to allow valid comparison across geographic regions or demographic groups with differing age pyramids.
Primary Locator: NCHS Age Standardization Statistical Reports (Anderson & Rosenberg)
Underlying Sources: [SRC-CDC-NVSS-2022-FINAL] [SRC-CDC-WISQARS-2024]

Module 5 Epidemiological Metrics

5. Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL) & Derived Measures

How premature mortality is quantified through YPLL-75, economic and societal impact modeling, and the limitations of derived statistical indicators.

30-Second Takeaway

YPLL subtracts age at death from a benchmark (typically age 75). A death at age 20 equals 55 YPLL; a death at age 70 equals 5 YPLL. Because suicide disproportionately affects youth and middle-aged adults, it generates massive YPLL compared to late-life diseases.

Terms Not to Conflate

Term A Term B Scientific Distinction
Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL-75) Total Mortality Count YPLL weights each death by how many years prior to age 75 it occurred; mortality count treats every death as 1 unit regardless of age.
YPLL Rate per 100k Crude Death Rate per 100k YPLL rate standardizes total lost years across population sizes; crude death rate standardizes event counts.
Permitted Claims & Primary Citation Locators

Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL-75) demonstrates the profound societal burden of suicide by illustrating that deaths among youth and young adults result in decades of unlived potential.

[DRAFT - OPEN] CLM-YPLL-LOGIC: Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL-75) measures premature mortality by calculating the difference between an individual age at death and a standard benchmark (usually age 75), highlighting the disproportionate societal and economic burden when mortality occurs in younger cohorts.
Primary Locator: CDC WISQARS Fatal Injury Reports, Years of Potential Life Lost Module
Underlying Sources: [SRC-CDC-WISQARS-2024]

Module 7 Epidemiological Metrics

7. Small Numbers, Cell Suppression & False Precision

Statistical instability in low-incidence events, federal cell suppression rules (n < 10), relative standard error (RSE) thresholds, and avoiding misleading claims based on small-sample noise.

30-Second Takeaway

If a small county has 1 suicide in year one and 3 in year two, the rate appears to "triple (+200%)," but this is random noise in a small cell. CDC suppresses counts under 10 for privacy and flags counts under 20 (RSE >= 30%) as unstable.

Terms Not to Conflate

Term A Term B Scientific Distinction
Statistical Instability (High RSE) Real Epidemiological Trend Instability occurs when small event numbers cause extreme percentage swings purely by chance; a trend requires stable, statistically significant multi-year changes.
Data Suppression for Privacy Data Suppression for Reliability Privacy suppression protects individual confidentiality (counts 1–9); reliability suppression prevents publishing misleading unstable rates.
Permitted Claims & Primary Citation Locators

Multi-year data pooling is necessary when analyzing suicide rates in small rural counties or disaggregated racial subgroups to achieve statistical reliability.

[DRAFT - OPEN] CLM-SMALL-CELL-INSTABILITY: When calculating suicide rates for small demographic subgroups or low-population rural counties with fewer than 20 recorded events per year, rates fluctuate drastically due to random variation, requiring suppression or multi-year pooling to avoid manufactured false precision.
Primary Locator: CDC NCHS Guideline for Reporting and Assessing Data Reliability
Underlying Sources: [SRC-CDC-NVSS-2022-FINAL] [SRC-CDC-WISQARS-2024]

Module 8 Intervention Science

8. Caring Contacts: Evidence by Endpoint, Dose & Limitation

The landmark Motto trials (Motto 1976; Motto & Bostrom 2001), mechanism of unconditional non-demanding contact, follow-up trajectories over 15 years, and limits across modern digital modalities (SMS, email).

30-Second Takeaway

In Motto 1976 and Motto & Bostrom 2001 landmark inpatient trials, sending 24 brief, unconditional, non-demanding letters over 5 years to treatment-refusing patients significantly lowered suicide mortality in years 1–4. Modern automated texts, however, vary widely in efficacy.

Terms Not to Conflate

Term A Term B Scientific Distinction
Unconditional Caring Contact Clinical Compliance Follow-up Caring contacts make zero demands, require no response, and offer pure empathy; compliance calls demand appointment scheduling or medication checks.
Long-term Multi-year Schedule (24 letters) Single Modern Automated SMS Motto protocol delivered sustained, spaced contact over 5 years; single automated check-ins have distinct, often smaller effect sizes.
Permitted Claims & Primary Citation Locators

In Motto landmark RCTs (1976, 2001), sustained, unconditional caring letters were associated with reduced suicide mortality in treatment-refusing psychiatric patients over early follow-up years.

[DRAFT - OPEN] CLM-CARING-CONTACTS-MOTTO-RCT: Jerome Motto landmark randomized controlled trials demonstrated that brief, unconditional, non-demanding caring letters sent over a multi-year schedule significantly reduced suicide mortality among high-risk psychiatric patients post-discharge in the first 2–4 years compared to contact-free controls (p < 0.05).
Primary Locator: Motto (1976), SLTB 6(4):248–257; Motto & Bostrom (2001), Psychiatric Services 52(6):828–833
Underlying Sources: [SRC-MOTTO-1976] [SRC-MOTTO-BOSTROM-2001]
[DRAFT - OPEN] CLM-CARING-CONTACTS-MODALITY-LIMIT: Caring contacts cannot be assumed to produce universal mortality reduction across all modalities (e.g., automated one-way SMS vs personalized letters); clinical trials show varying efficacy on nonfatal re-attempt endpoints, and caring contacts do not replace comprehensive crisis care or clinical safety planning.

📖 Comprehensive Epidemiological & Surveillance Glossary

Term Domain Operational Definition & Distinction
Age-Adjusted Mortality Rate Metrics A weighted average of age-specific death rates calculated by applying observed age-specific rates to a standard population distribution (2000 U.S. Standard Population). Eliminates confounding when comparing populations with differing age structures.
Crude Death Rate Metrics The total number of observed deaths divided by the total resident population, expressed per 100,000 people. Represents the actual unadjusted event frequency in a specific geographic area.
Leading Cause Rank Surveillance An ordinal ranking of mortality causes relative to all competing causes within a specific demographic cohort. Suicide ranks high (#2) in youth primarily because chronic diseases are rare, not because youth mortality rates exceed older adult rates.
Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL-75) Metrics An epidemiological measure of premature mortality calculated by subtracting the exact age at death from benchmark age 75. Emphasizes the societal and economic burden of deaths occurring in young and middle-aged cohorts.
Direct Standardization Metrics The mathematical process of applying group-specific rates to a single standard population distribution to generate comparable summary rates independent of demographic differences.
Relative Standard Error (RSE) Statistics A measure of statistical precision calculated as the standard error divided by the estimate, multiplied by 100. CDC guidelines flag estimates with RSE >= 30% as statistically unstable.
Cell Suppression Surveillance The policy of withholding small cell counts (typically fewer than 10 events) to protect individual privacy and prevent misleading interpretations derived from random variance.
National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) Surveillance The intergovernmental federal system managed by CDC NCHS that compiles complete legal death certificate data from all 50 states, DC, and territories.
National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) Surveillance A nationally representative annual household survey of the civilian noninstitutionalized population aged 12+ measuring self-reported suicidal thoughts, plans, and attempts.
Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS) Surveillance A biennial school-based survey conducted by the CDC among 9th–12th grade public and private high school students tracking self-reported health risk behaviors.
WISQARS Surveillance CDC Web-based Injury Statistics Query and Reporting System, an interactive database providing fatal injury, nonfatal emergency department, and cost-of-injury estimates.
National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS) Surveillance A state-based surveillance system combining death certificates, coroner/medical examiner toxicology reports, and law enforcement incident narratives into a single unified incident record.
Positive Predictive Value (PPV) Psychometrics The probability that a person flagged as high risk by a screening instrument will actually experience the target outcome. In rare events like suicide, systematic meta-analyses (Carter et al. 2017) demonstrate a pooled PPV of 5.5%.
Sensitivity Psychometrics The proportion of actual positive cases that are correctly identified by a screening tool.
Specificity Psychometrics The proportion of actual negative cases that are correctly identified as negative by a screening tool.
Triage Screener Psychometrics A brief standardized questionnaire (e.g., C-SSRS, PHQ-9 Item 9) designed for broad detection to prompt clinical conversation, not to predict individual suicide behavior.
Caring Contacts Intervention An evidence-based suicide prevention strategy consisting of brief, unconditional, non-demanding expressions of care sent at spaced intervals post-discharge (Motto 1976; Motto & Bostrom 2001).
American Indian Life Skills (AILS) Intervention A 30-lesson culturally grounded classroom curriculum developed in partnership with tribal community elders to build communication, problem-solving, and peer help-giving skills in Indigenous youth.
Indigenous Data Sovereignty Governance The inherent right of sovereign Tribal Nations to govern, own, manage, approve, and protect data collection and research concerning their peoples and lands.
Contract Health Service Delivery Areas (CHSDA) Surveillance Geographic county designations where Indian Health Service (IHS) data linkage studies are conducted to correct for death certificate racial misclassification.
Racial Misclassification Surveillance The erroneous recording of an individual race on legal death certificates by coroners, funeral directors, or hospital staff, undercounting American Indian/Alaska Native suicide mortality by 15%–33%.
Carrier Wireless Georouting Architecture A privacy-preserving telecommunications protocol implemented in 988 that routes calls to the nearest local crisis center based on mobile cell-tower region without tracking GPS coordinates.
Werther Effect Communication An observed increase in suicide rates or copycat behavior following sensationalized, graphic, or romanticized media coverage of a suicide death.
Papageno Effect Communication An observed increase in crisis help-seeking, hope, and resilience following media stories highlighting coping, overcoming suicidal crises, and available support resources.
Postvention Intervention Organized community, clinical, and bereavement interventions provided after a suicide death to support loss survivors, facilitate grieving, and mitigate contagion.
Four-Tier Claim Labeling Governance An evidence governance taxonomy categorizing assertions into VERIFIED (direct peer-reviewed/vital statistics proof), STRONG INFERENCE (multi-system logical deduction), HYPOTHESIS (testable theoretical model), and OPEN (unresolved empirical gap).
